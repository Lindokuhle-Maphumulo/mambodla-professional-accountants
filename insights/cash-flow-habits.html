5 cash flow habits every business should master

Healthy cash flow is not only about how much a business earns. It is about when money arrives, when obligations fall due and whether management can see pressure early enough to act.

10 Sep 2026 4 min read Mambodla Professional Accountants

Healthy cash flow is one of the clearest signs that a business can meet its obligations, respond to opportunities and absorb unexpected pressure.

Profit matters, but profit and cash are not the same thing. A business can report a profit while still struggling to pay suppliers, salaries or tax on time if money is tied up in debtors, stock or poorly timed expenses. Strong cash-flow habits help management understand not only how much the business earns, but when money actually moves.

01

Know what cash is coming in — and when

Cash-flow management starts with visibility. Business owners should know which customers owe money, when those balances are expected to be settled and whether actual collections are matching expectations.

A sales figure does not become usable cash until the customer pays. Reviewing debtor ageing, payment patterns and overdue accounts regularly helps management distinguish between revenue that exists on paper and cash that is genuinely available.

This is especially important where a small number of customers make up a large portion of turnover. A delay from one major customer can quickly affect the rest of the business.

02

Build a realistic short-term cash forecast

A useful cash-flow forecast does not need to be complicated. It needs to be current, realistic and connected to the decisions the business is making.

Start with expected cash receipts, then map the payments that must leave the business: salaries, suppliers, tax, rent, loan repayments and other operating commitments. Looking several weeks or months ahead can reveal periods where cash may become tight before the pressure arrives.

The purpose is not to predict every rand perfectly. It is to give management enough warning to act.

Key takeaway

Cash problems are easier to manage when they are visible before they become urgent.

03

Tighten the gap between invoicing and collection

Many businesses focus heavily on making sales and not enough on converting those sales into cash.

Simple disciplines can make a meaningful difference: issue invoices promptly, make payment terms clear, follow up overdue balances consistently and resolve disputes quickly. Where appropriate, ask for deposits or staged payments on larger projects so that the business is not carrying the full funding burden itself.

The objective is not aggressive collection. It is predictable collection.

04

Match spending to cash priorities

Every expense competes for the same cash pool.

Strong cash-flow management means distinguishing between necessary operating costs, growth investments and spending that can be delayed or reduced without harming the business. It also means understanding when large annual or irregular payments are due so they do not arrive as surprises.

This does not mean cutting costs blindly. Reducing an expense that protects revenue, quality or capacity can create a bigger problem later. The better question is whether each significant cash outflow supports a clear business priority.

05

Keep tax and other obligations out of working cash

One of the easiest cash-flow mistakes is treating money that will eventually be paid to SARS, employees or other third parties as though it belongs to the business.

Amounts collected or withheld for VAT, PAYE and similar obligations should be planned for deliberately. The same applies to provisional tax and other known commitments that may not be payable immediately but will still require cash at a future date.

A practical cash-flow review should ask:

  • What cash is available today?
  • What money is expected in over the next few weeks?
  • Which customers are overdue?
  • What fixed and irregular payments are approaching?
  • What tax or payroll obligations need to be protected?
  • What spending can be deferred if cash becomes tighter than expected?

Good businesses create room for these obligations before the deadline arrives.

Mambodla Perspective

Cash flow is not only a finance issue. It is a decision-making issue.

When management understands the timing of cash, the business can make better choices about spending, growth, pricing, credit and risk. Strong cash-flow habits create options. Weak visibility removes them.
