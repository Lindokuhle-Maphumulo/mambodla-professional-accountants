Understanding the 2026 tax changes

South Africa’s 2026 tax changes introduce inflation-linked relief for individuals and meaningful threshold changes for smaller businesses. The important question is how those changes affect tax, payroll and planning decisions.

16 Sep 2026 4 min read Mambodla Professional Accountants

South Africa’s 2026 tax changes affect more than annual returns. They influence payroll, VAT registration, cash-flow planning and the way smaller businesses assess which tax regime may be appropriate.

For business owners, the useful question is therefore not simply what changed. It is whether those changes alter an existing obligation, create a planning opportunity or require a process to be updated. Reviewing the position early helps prevent a technical change from becoming a compliance problem later.

01

Personal tax thresholds have moved with inflation

The 2026/27 personal income-tax brackets and rebates were adjusted by 3.4% for inflation, the first inflationary adjustment since the 2023/24 tax year. For taxpayers under 65, the tax threshold increased to R99 000.

For businesses, these changes matter most where owners, directors and employees receive remuneration. Payroll calculations, remuneration planning and provisional-tax estimates should reflect the current tax year rather than relying on prior-year assumptions.

The change may look modest in isolation, but using outdated tables can create avoidable differences between tax deducted during the year and the final liability assessed later.

02

The VAT registration thresholds are significantly higher

One of the most significant changes for smaller businesses is the increase in the compulsory VAT registration threshold from R1 million to R2.3 million in annual taxable supplies from 1 April 2026. The voluntary-registration threshold also increased from R50 000 to R120 000.

SARS is administering registration and deregistration requests using the higher thresholds. Businesses below the new compulsory threshold should nevertheless avoid treating deregistration as an automatic decision.

A VAT decision can affect pricing, input-tax recovery, customers, administration and cash flow. The right response depends on the nature of the business rather than the threshold alone.

Key takeaway

A higher threshold can reduce compliance pressure, but it does not replace the need to review what is appropriate for the business.

03

Turnover tax now reaches more micro-businesses

The turnover-tax regime for qualifying micro-businesses was also expanded. The annual turnover limit increased from R1 million to R2.3 million, while the tax-free turnover band increased to R600 000.

Turnover tax is designed as a simplified system for qualifying micro-businesses, but eligibility and suitability still need to be considered carefully. A lower administrative burden does not automatically make it the best option for every business.

Owners should compare the effect of the regime with the business’s actual margins, structure, growth plans and VAT position before making a change.

04

Company tax is unchanged, but small-business rules still matter

The standard corporate income-tax rate remains 27% for years of assessment ending from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027.

Small Business Corporations continue to use graduated rates where the relevant requirements are met. For the current period, the first R99 000 of taxable income is taxed at 0%, followed by graduated bands before the 27% rate applies above the upper threshold.

The important word is qualifying. SBC treatment is not available simply because a company is small. Ownership, gross-income and activity requirements still need to be satisfied. Businesses should confirm their status before relying on preferential rates in forecasts or provisional-tax calculations.

05

Payroll and tax processes should be reviewed now

The 2026/27 PAYE deduction tables took effect from 1 March 2026. Employers should therefore ensure that payroll systems and calculations use the current tables and rebates.

This is also a useful point in the year to review the wider tax position rather than focusing on one rate or threshold in isolation.

  • Has the business’s VAT registration position changed under the new threshold?
  • Could turnover tax now be relevant, and would it actually suit the business?
  • Is payroll using the 2026/27 PAYE tables and current employee information?
  • Do provisional-tax forecasts still reflect expected taxable income?
  • Does the company qualify for the tax treatment being used?
  • Are accounting records and supporting documents complete enough to support returns and calculations?

The objective is not to change something simply because a threshold moved. It is to confirm that the business’s tax position still matches its current size, structure and circumstances.

Mambodla Perspective

Tax changes create value only when they are translated into decisions.

A change in a rate or threshold may appear technical, but its effect can reach pricing, payroll, cash flow and business planning. Businesses that review changes early are better placed to make deliberate decisions, maintain compliance and avoid unnecessary surprises.
